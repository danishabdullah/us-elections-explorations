Democrats ran on Sanders's message in 2026, endorsed or not, and ran far ahead of Harris

A widely shared chart split 2026 Senate Democrats into the five Bernie Sanders endorsed and everyone else. That split misses the story. The other eleven ran on his message just as hard: the cost of living, billionaires, corporate power. On policy they moved toward him without going all the way: a public option rather than Medicare for All, a $15 minimum wage rather than $17. On Gaza, five of them broke with the Biden–Harris line and would restrict arms to Israel. And 15 of the 16 ran ahead of Harris, by a median of 16 points. A midterm under Trump explains part of that gain; with nearly everyone on the same message, these races can't say how much.

Endorsed or not, they ran on Sanders's message. On policy, most went part of the way

All 16 candidates, measured the same way from their own words, votes and pledges: three campaign-message themes, four Sanders policies, their stance on Israel and Gaza, and endorsements from progressive groups. Read down any message column: it's nearly solid, whoever endorsed them.

Message: ten of the eleven ran on the cost of living, and nine each ran against billionaires and against corporate power, centrists such as Cooper included. Policy: none backed Medicare for All, but eight backed a public option or Medicare buy-in, and ten backed raising the federal minimum wage, mostly to $15. Most also refused corporate PAC money or want Citizens United overturned. Israel and Gaza: Osborn would cut off aid to Israel outright, and Ossoff, Talarico, Turek and Hamilton would restrict arms while condemning how the war was fought. A faint dot means we found nothing either way.

The Israel and Gaza stance is one judgment per candidate from their whole record, weighted to the 2025–26 campaign, on a 0–4 scale of distance from Sanders's position: 4 with Sanders or beyond, 3 restrict arms and sharply condemn the conduct, 2 critical but no arms limits, 1 supportive with concern, 0 unconditional support. Compared with the last race for the same seat, they were roughly average

The arrow runs from the previous November result for each seat to 2026. Seats Democrats already won had much less room to swing, so each swing is compared with the median of similar seats. On this view El-Sayed and Flanagan lead their group, and Jackson and Nixon sit near the median of theirs.

Most previous races were in 2020, a presidential year. Georgia uses the Nov 2020 general; Ossoff won the seat in the Jan 2021 runoff. Nebraska uses the 2024 special for this seat.

Against their own past races, Osborn and Peltola were about as far ahead as usual. Ossoff, Cooper and Brown gained the most

Six of the 16 have run statewide before, which gives a baseline that belongs to the candidate rather than the seat: how far each ran ahead of their party's presidential candidate in that earlier race, and how far ahead of Harris in 2026.

Osborn and Peltola ran 14 and 11 points ahead of Harris on the same 2024 ballot; in 2026 they were 14 and 13 ahead. Running ahead of Harris wasn't new for them. Brown roughly doubled his edge, from 8 points to 16. Ossoff and Cooper ran within a few points of Biden in 2020 and more than 20 ahead of Harris in 2026. Markey, the one Sanders pick here, went from level with Biden to 6 ahead of Harris.

Every earlier race shared a ballot with the presidential race it is compared with; 2026 had no presidential race, so “now” compares with Harris's 2024 result. Osborn is an independent; he is compared with Harris. Alaska uses first-choice votes. Earlier results are from the Clerk of the House, the FEC and North Carolina's certified canvass, each matched to the state's own file.

When Sanders began endorsing, his picks were in competitive races as often as the others. The other races tightened later

A rating made after an endorsement can't show how a race was chosen: the endorsement, and the nominee it helped pick, can move the rating. So these are Sabato's Crystal Ball ratings over time, from its first ratings in February 2025 to its last on Nov 2, 2026.

Before Sanders's first endorsement, of El-Sayed on Apr 17, 2025, three of his five eventual picks were in competitive races (Lean or Toss-up): Michigan, Minnesota and Maine. So were three of the other eleven. His picks' ratings then barely moved. Seven of the other eleven started out Safe or Likely Republican and moved toward Democrats, mostly during 2026. By the final ratings, seven of the eleven were competitive, against two of the five picks.

So Sanders didn't steer around close races. What his picks shared was a fight inside the party: four of the five won a seriously contested primary, against three of the eleven others, and three of the five endorsements came during a primary. Two, Jackson and Nixon, came only in the final months.

Two independent collections of every rating change agreed on all 38 changes; for 2025 they relied on syndicated copies and same-day reports because the Crystal Ball's site blocked automated access, so a change made and reversed within 2025 could be missing. Jackson's date is a rally in Bangor; Sanders made no dated written endorsement.

Among candidates who all ran on his message, going further toward Sanders didn't bring better results

This can't tell you whether Sanders's message paid off. Fifteen of the 16 candidates ran on at least two of its three themes, and 15 of the 16 ran ahead of Harris, by a median of 16 points. With almost nobody running without the message, there's nothing to compare against.

What it can test is narrower: among candidates who all ran on that message, did going further toward Sanders's policies or his Israel stance help? Against the Harris expectation, candidates closer to his policies did worse (r −0.58). Most of that is the five picks: among the other eleven it weakens to −0.39, which chance alone could produce. Israel and Gaza stance shows no link among the eleven. Against each seat's last result, neither tracks performance.

r is the correlation across all 16 races; the range is a 90% bootstrap interval; p is from a permutation test (20,000 shuffles). Policy counts a full Sanders position as 1 and a step toward it (public option; a raise below $17) as ½. Unclear counts as 0.

Method and sources

Margin is the Democratic (or independent) candidate's votes minus the Republican's, divided by all votes for the office. Nebraska 2026 had no Democrat; Dan Osborn ran as an independent. Maine and Alaska use first-choice counts. The 2026 counts are unofficial: Alaska was about 74% counted, Maine about 94%, Mississippi about 97% and Ohio about 98% at retrieval. The expectation line is an ordinary least-squares fit across all 16 races.

Every count comes from an official state source, FEC, a state's electoral certificate, the AP results feed or Decision Desk HQ, and each was checked against a second, independent source. Full citations are in data/results.csv and data/sources.csv.

Alignment codes follow a scheme written before collection (data/alignment/SCHEMA.md), revised in the open where it measured wording instead of positions; every revision is dated there. Values need the candidate's own words, vote, pledge, a named campaign spokesperson, or the endorsing group's own list; opposition research never decides a value. Positions evolve, so the latest clear position counts. Health care and the minimum wage are graded (Medicare for All / public option / neither; $17 or more / a lower or unnamed raise / no), replacing yes-or-no items that only matched Sanders's exact bills. Israel and Gaza is one assessed stance from each candidate's whole record, replacing four keyword items that missed positions stated in other words. Each dataset was coded twice independently and disagreements were resolved at the sources (scripts/reconcile_*.py). Dropped after collection, with reasons: the Green New Deal (a 2019–20 label 14 of 16 never mentioned) and Justice Democrats and the Progressive Caucus PAC (they endorsed only House candidates). Final values with evidence and sources are in data/alignment/alignment_final.csv; scores in data/alignment/scores.csv.
